1. Problem Definition

Axis Insurance wants a detailed statistical understanding of customers enrolled in its MediClaim policy. Using the AxisInsurance.csv dataset (age, sex, bmi, children, smoker, region, charges) we will:
  1. Explore the dataset (Exploratory Data Analysis — univariate & bivariate).
  2. H1. Prove/disprove that medical claims by smokers are greater than those by non-smokers.
  3. H2. Prove/disprove that the BMI of females is different from that of males.
  4. H3. Does the smoking habit of customers depend on their region?
  5. H4. Is the mean BMI of women with 0, 1, and 2 children the same?

Significance level α = 0.05 for all tests.

5. Hypothesis Test 1 — Do Smokers Have Higher Claims?

Visual analysis (above): smokers' boxplot is shifted far upward. Hypotheses (one-tailed):
  • $H_0$: $\mu_{\text{smoker}} \le \mu_{\text{non-smoker}}$
  • $H_a$: $\mu_{\text{smoker}} > \mu_{\text{non-smoker}}$
Test selection: Two-sample independent t-test (Welch's, one-tailed) — comparing means of two independent groups, sample sizes are large so CLT lets us use t-test even though charges is skewed.
In []:
smoker_ch = df.loc[df.smoker=='yes','charges']
nonsm_ch  = df.loc[df.smoker=='no','charges']
print('n(smoker)=', len(smoker_ch), '  mean=', smoker_ch.mean().round(2))
print('n(non)   =', len(nonsm_ch),  '  mean=', nonsm_ch.mean().round(2))
t_stat, p_two = stats.ttest_ind(smoker_ch, nonsm_ch, equal_var=False)
p_one = p_two / 2 if t_stat > 0 else 1 - p_two/2
print(f't = {t_stat:.3f}    one-tailed p = {p_one:.3e}')
Out:
n(smoker)= 274   mean= 32050.23
n(non)   = 1064   mean= 8434.27
t = 32.752    one-tailed p = 2.945e-103
Inference: p-value = 2.94e-103 < 0.05 → reject $H_0$. Smokers' average medical claim (≈ \$32,050) is statistically significantly higher than non-smokers' (≈ \$8,434). The data strongly supports that smokers file higher claims.

6. Hypothesis Test 2 — Is BMI Different Between Females and Males?

Hypotheses (two-tailed):
  • $H_0$: $\mu_{\text{female BMI}} = \mu_{\text{male BMI}}$
  • $H_a$: $\mu_{\text{female BMI}} \ne \mu_{\text{male BMI}}$
Test selection: Two-sample independent t-test (two-tailed, Welch's).

Inference: p-value = 0.090 > 0.05 → fail to reject $H_0$. There is no statistically significant difference between female and male BMI. The visual difference in boxplots is within sampling variability.

8. Hypothesis Test 4 — Mean BMI of Women by Number of Children (0/1/2)

Hypotheses:
  • $H_0$: $\mu_{0} = \mu_{1} = \mu_{2}$ (mean BMI is equal across the three groups)
  • $H_a$: At least one group's mean BMI differs.
Test selection: One-way ANOVA (comparing >2 group means on a continuous variable).

1Do smokers have higher charges?Yes — strongly (p ≈ 0)Re-price smoker premiums; invest in cessation programmes — the ROI is highest here.
2Does BMI differ by sex?No (p ≈ 0.09)Don't gender-segment BMI-based premiums; treat as unisex risk.
3Does smoking depend on region?No significant dependence (p ≈ 0.06)Run a uniform anti-smoking campaign nationally rather than over-indexing to one region.

Top business priorities:
  1. Tier premiums by smoker status — this is the only consumer attribute with a large, statistically validated impact on claims.
  2. Launch a wellness / BMI-management plan — average BMI is in the obese range, which interacts with smoking to drive cost.
  3. Avoid sex-based or region-based BMI pricing — there is no statistical support.
  4. Monitor age & BMI as continuous risk variables in pricing models rather than relying on demographic segments.
